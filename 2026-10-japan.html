FUKUOKA · Days 1-2 · arrival hub
Theme

Kyushu's biggest, most easygoing city. A food-and-neighbourhood place, light on big monuments, so it works as a gentle reset after the redeye.

How the days split

Day 1 (Sun) lands at 07:35, does the whole rail errand at Hakata in one visit, drops the bag, and uses the afternoon for Dazaifu plus the Kyushu National Museum, which is open on Sundays. Day 2 (Mon) is central Fukuoka as one west-to-east sweep, starting at Ohori Park and ending near the hostel, since the museum closes Mondays. You get the city again at the relaxed end, Days 11-12.

Route

Local food primer - Fukuoka (what is famous here)

Hakata ramen (tonkotsu): thin, straight noodles in a rich, milky broth made from boiled pork bones; the city's signature bowl. Motsunabe: a hotpot of beef or pork offal (intestines) simmered with cabbage and garlic chives. Mizutaki: a gentle, clear chicken hotpot. Mentaiko: spicy-marinated pollock fish roe (the eggs), eaten with rice or in pasta. Yatai: the open-air street-food stalls, mostly along the Nakasu riverbank, serving ramen, grilled skewers, and oden (bits simmered in broth). Umegae mochi: a grilled rice cake, crisp outside, with sweet red-bean paste inside (a Dazaifu snack). Sweet: Hakata Torimon, a soft cake with a milky white-bean filling; and amaou, a prized local strawberry. Non-alcoholic drink: Yame gyokuro, a top shaded green tea from Fukuoka's Yame area, mellow and a little sweet.

NAGASAKI · Days 3-4 · the trading-port city
Theme

Japan's old window on the world. A hilly harbour city of layered foreign-trade history and a food scene found nowhere else.

Lucky timing: the Nagasaki Kunchi festival (7-9 Oct), and Day 4 is its opening day

"Kunchi" (くんち) is Nagasaki's big autumn festival, held every year on 7, 8 and 9 October at Suwa Shrine and a few other venues around town. It is famous for its blend of Japanese, Chinese and Dutch influences, a legacy of Nagasaki's trading-port past. Each year a handful of neighbourhoods (the odori-cho, "dance towns") take turns performing in a rotation, so each one comes round only once every seven years. The signature acts are the ja-odori (a long dragon, carried and spun by a team) and elaborate wheeled floats (kasaboko and decorated "ships"). 2026 features six dance towns, including a dragon dance and the famous "Kokko-desho" float.

Background, in plain words

Reference only, parked at the end so it stays out of the way of the planning content. Quick context for the terms and places above.

The 2026 Kumamoto earthquake (28 Jul) and why it touches Days 5, 6 and 9

A magnitude 6.8-7.1 earthquake with maximum intensity 7 (the top of Japan's scale) hit Kumamoto Prefecture on 28 July 2026, killing dozens, mostly south of the city around Yatsushiro. Kumamoto City itself escaped major damage and runs normally: Kumamoto Castle lost about 40 stretches of wall again (it was still being repaired from the 2016 quake) but reopened its keep on 13 August, and Suizenji garden is open. The Kyushu Shinkansen, your legs 2, 3 and 5, was cut between Kumamoto and Shin-Minamata for 52 days and reopened along the full line on 18 September, for now at 70 km/h near Shin-Yatsushiro with fewer trains, which is already inside the times in "Getting around". Aftershocks continue (an intensity-4 one on 26 September). Practical meaning: check JR Kyushu's operating-status page and the castle's status page in the 1-3 Oct sweep and the evening before each Shinkansen leg; otherwise nothing changes.

Sports Day (Mon 12 Oct 2026)

A Japanese national holiday on the second Monday of October (it began to mark the 1964 Tokyo Olympics). Falling on a Monday, it makes a 3-day weekend, one of autumn's busiest domestic travel windows. Effect on us: bullet trains, the reserved sightseeing trains, and popular onsen towns and hotels all fill up. That is why the plan keeps you in one Kagoshima hotel across that weekend, booked the Tamatebako at its window, and takes a free reserved seat on the Sonic for the holiday Monday. One more rule: a museum that closes on Mondays shifts its closing day to Tuesday 13 Oct after a holiday Monday, which the 1-3 Oct sweep checks for Yufuin.

Gunkanjima "landing"

Gunkanjima ("Battleship Island," officially Hashima) is an abandoned coal-mining island about 18 km off Nagasaki; its concrete ruins and silhouette look like a warship, and it is now a UNESCO site. Tour boats go out; "landing" means the boat actually docks so you walk on a fixed observation deck, versus only circling the island. The dock faces open sea, so a safe landing is only possible on calm days (around 100 a year). You booked the cruise; the landing is the bonus if the water is calm, and the sea approach is the real payoff either way.

Onsen vs the "hells"

An onsen is a natural hot-spring bath you soak in, usually at a ryokan (a traditional inn) that also serves a multi-course kaiseki dinner. Beppu's "hells" (jigoku) are the opposite: geothermal ponds near 100°C, in vivid colours (cobalt blue, blood red), far too hot to enter. They are a viewing circuit sold on one combined ticket. So in Beppu and Yufuin you both soak (onsen) and sightsee (the hells).

Sakurajima and the ferry

Sakurajima is one of Japan's most active volcanoes, sitting in the bay directly across from Kagoshima city, and it regularly dusts the city with ash. You reach it by a short public ferry (15 min, every 15 to 20 min, around the clock) because it is across the water. You cannot climb it (a safety zone bans entry within 2 km of the crater), so you take it in from observatories like Yunohira, linked by the Island View loop bus, and from the flat lava-coast trail. Since 1914 it has technically been a peninsula: that eruption's lava joined it to the far shore.

Sightseeing trains (and why they must be reserved)

Beyond ordinary trains, JR Kyushu runs themed "sightseeing trains" with designed interiors and big panoramic windows; the ride itself is the attraction. Two are on this trip: the black-and-white Ibusuki no Tamatebako (to the sand baths and back, Day 8, leg 4) and the forest-green Yufuin no Mori (Yufuin to Hakata, Day 11, leg 7). Bullet train, limited express, and the "relay"

The Shinkansen is the high-speed bullet train. A "limited express" (like the Sonic to Beppu, or the Relay Kamome) is a fast ordinary train, a notch slower but still quick. One quirk: the Nagasaki bullet line is not yet joined to the main network, so Hakata to Nagasaki means the Relay Kamome express to Takeo-Onsen, then a 3-minute change across the platform (the "relay") onto the Kamome bullet train for the last stretch. Nagasaki to Kumamoto does the same in reverse, plus a second change at Shin-Tosu onto the Kyushu Shinkansen.

Rail pass and reserved seats, in one line

A rail pass is prepaid unlimited travel on JR trains for a set number of consecutive days. A "reserved seat" is a guaranteed assigned seat; "non-reserved" is first-come in open cars. With the All-Kyushu pass you board non-reserved cars freely. Reserved seats are free and unlimited at a station counter in Japan, but booking them online in advance from home costs extra (¥1,500 a section on the sightseeing trains, ¥1,000 on bullet and limited express), so the plan paid that only for the Tamatebako and takes the other five free at Hakata on 4 Oct. Nishitetsu (the Dazaifu line)

Fukuoka has a private railway, Nishitetsu, separate from JR. Dazaifu sits on it, so the JR rail pass does not cover that trip; it is a small separate fare. Nishitetsu also runs the airport bus and the city buses. Noted so the pass math stays clear.

Why Nagasaki has Dutch and church history

For about 200 years Japan was largely closed to the world. Nagasaki was the one exception: Dejima, a small fan-shaped artificial island, was the only place Dutch traders could live, with a Chinese quarter nearby. After Japan reopened in the 1850s, Western merchants built hillside mansions (now Glover Garden) and churches (Oura, Japan's oldest). That history is why Nagasaki feels different, and why its food (champon, castella) does too.

Zen, tea, udon: the Hakata temple quarter

The two temples on Day 11 are where Japan's Zen Buddhism starts. Zen is the meditation-centred school of Buddhism; its founder in Japan, the monk Eisai, studied in China and opened Shofukuji in 1195 as the country's first Zen temple, bringing tea seeds back with him. A generation later the monk Enni founded Jotenji next door and is credited with bringing the milling techniques for udon and soba noodles and for steamed buns, which is why the udon lunch in the station belongs to the same afternoon.

Daimyo gardens, and what Suizenji and Sengan-en actually are

Until 1868 Japan was "feudal": the country was split into territories, each run by a powerful regional lord (a daimyo) who controlled the land and the samurai under him, all beneath a top military ruler (the shogun). A "daimyo garden" is a large landscape garden a lord built beside his residence, made for strolling. Suizenji (full name Suizenji Jojuen) is the name of one such garden in Kumamoto, not a person. Its quirk: it shrinks scenery from the old Tokaido highway (the historic road linking Edo, today's Tokyo, with Kyoto, lined with 53 post towns) into miniature inside the garden, including a small grassy mound shaped like Mt Fuji. Sengan-en is the name of the Shimazu family's villa-and-garden estate in Kagoshima. It uses "borrowed scenery" (shakkei): instead of walling itself off, it frames the real Sakurajima volcano across the bay so the volcano reads as part of the garden. Shoko Shuseikan at its gate is the factory the same family built in 1865 to make Western machines, now their museum.

Why the Meiji Restoration keeps coming up in Kagoshima

This is here only because three of your Kagoshima stops make more sense with one fact. Until 1868 Japan was run by a military government (the shogun), with the emperor a figurehead. Kagoshima, then the Satsuma domain ruled by the Shimazu lord family, led the rebellion that toppled the shogun and handed power back to the emperor, the upheaval that launched Japan's rapid modernisation, known as the "Meiji Restoration." That one thread links your stops: Sengan-en and Shoko Shuseikan are the Shimazu family's estate and factory, and Shiroyama hill is where the last samurai revolt made its final stand a few years later. (The Reimeikan history museum tells the fuller story, but you've dropped it from the plan, since these three already carry it; keep it only as a wet-weather fallback.) If that history does not grab you, Shiroyama's view alone still earns the stop.

Kumamon

Kumamon is Kumamoto Prefecture's mascot, a round black bear with red cheeks created in 2010 to promote the region when the Kyushu Shinkansen opened. He became Japan's most successful "yuru-chara" (a soft, deliberately goofy regional character), earning billions of yen in licensing, and has an "office" in Kumamoto with a stage where he appears on a published schedule. It is silly, and it is a genuinely Japanese phenomenon, which is why it fills a half-hour on Day 5.
